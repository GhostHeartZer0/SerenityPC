### Summary
*   **As a physical transition:** It moves at the **speed of light**.
*   **As a geometric projection (shadows):** It can move **faster than light**, but it carries no information.

### Prompt:
```text
where does rain fall up?
```

**Score:** 10.0/10

**Response:**
```text
Rain does not "fall up" in the traditional sense of gravity being reversed, as gravity always pulls liquid water toward the center of the Earth. However, there are specific meteorological and physical phenomena that can make it *appear* as though rain is moving upward:

**1. Strong Updrafts (Storms)**
In severe thunderstorms or supercells, there are powerful upward currents of air called **updrafts**. These can be strong enough to push raindrops back up into the clouds. This is actually how hail forms; the updrafts keep the water droplets suspended and push them higher into freezing altitudes where they freeze and grow before eventually becoming too heavy and falling.

**2. Orographic Lift (Wind hitting mountains)**
When wind hits a steep mountain or cliff, it is forced rapidly upward. If it is raining, the wind can catch the raindrops and blow them vertically up the side of the mountain, creating a "rain curtain" that moves upward.

**3. The "Rain-Wind" Effect (Turbulence)**
Near the ground, strong gusts of wind or turbulence (such as those caused by buildings or trees) can create localized vortices that kick rain droplets upward momentarily.

**4. Evaporation**
While not "rain" in the liquid sense, water "falls up" in the form of **water vapor** through evaporation. The sun heats the ocean and land, turning liquid water into gas, which rises into the atmosphere to form clouds.

**Summary:**
Rain only "falls up" when the **upward force of wind** (updrafts) is stronger than the **downward force of gravity**.
```

In the kitchen, a banana is classified as a **fruit**. However, if you are eating a green, unripe plantain, it is often treated and cooked as a **starchy vegetable**.
