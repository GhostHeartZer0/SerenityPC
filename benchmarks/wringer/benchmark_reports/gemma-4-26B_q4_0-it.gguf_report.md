You are simply changing which part of the surface is being blocked. Because no physical object or signal is traveling from one side of the shadow to the other, it does not violate Einstein’s theory of relativity.

```text
### Summary
*   **The speed of light:** $c$ (The speed at which "darkness" propagates when a light is turned off).
*   **The speed of a shadow:** Can appear to exceed $c$ because it is a moving pattern, not a physical object.
```

```text
### 1. The Botanical Answer (The Fruit)
Botanically speaking, a banana is a **berry**. 
In botany, a berry is a fleshy fruit produced from a single ovary of a single flower. Bananas meet all the technical criteria: they have a fleshy pulp, contain seeds (though modern ones are sterile), and develop from a single ovary.

However, the "plant" we call a banana tree is not actually a tree. It is a **large herbaceous perennial**. Because it lacks a woody stem and is instead made of tightly packed, leaf-like structures, botanists classify it as an **herb**.

When you eat a banana from a grocery store (the **Cavendish** variety), you are eating a genetic clone.

Because these bananas are sterile and have no seeds, they cannot reproduce on their own. Every Cavendish banana in the world is a clone of a single parent plant, propagated by humans through "suckers" (offsets from the base of the plant).

### 3. The Culinary Answer (The Food)
In the kitchen, we categorize food by how it is used rather than its biological origin. To a chef, a banana is a **fruit**, characterized by its high starch/sugar content, soft texture, and use in desserts or snacks.

### 4. The Evolutionary Answer (The History)
What we eat today is a "domesticated" version of a wild plant. 
Wild bananas are small, green, and filled with large, hard, inedible seeds. The "true" ancestor of the modern banana was a wild variety that humans selected for its lack of seeds and its sweeter flesh. This means the banana we know is a highly engineered biological product—a partnership between human intervention and plant evolution.

*   **Botanically:** A berry from a giant herb.
```

```text
To understand the difference between merging and rebasing, it is best to think of them as two different ways of recording the history of a project: one preserves the **chronological reality** of when work happened, while the other preserves a **clean story** of how features were implemented.

---

### 1. Git Merging
Merging takes the contents of a source branch and integrates them into a target branch via a **"Merge Commit."**

*   **How it works:** Git finds the "common ancestor" (the last time the two branches were identical) and performs a three-way merge. It creates a new commit that has two parents, tying the two histories together.
*   **The Result:** A non-linear, "diamond-shaped" history graph that shows exactly when a feature branch was branched off and when it was brought back in.

**Pros:**
*   **Traceability:** You can see the exact chronological order of events and when a feature was integrated.
*   **Safety:** It is a non-destructive operation. Existing commits are never altered.
*   **Simplicity:** It is easier to resolve conflicts because the history remains intact.

**Cons:**
*   **Clutter:** In busy repositories, the history graph can become a "tangled web" of merge commits and crisscrossing lines, making it hard to read the high-level project evolution.

---

### 2. Git Rebasing
Rebasing "re-writes" the project history by moving a sequence of commits to a new base commit.

*   **How it works:** Git takes all the commits from your feature branch, temporarily sets them aside, moves your branch's starting point to the tip of the target branch (the latest `main`), and then **re-applies** your commits one by one onto that new tip.
*   **The Result:** A perfectly linear history. It looks as if you developed your feature on the very latest version of the project, even if you actually started weeks ago.

**Pros:**
*   **Cleanliness:** The project history is a single straight line. This makes `git log` and `git bisect` (used for finding bugs) much easier to navigate.
*   **Eliminates Noise:** You avoid the "Merge branch 'main' into feature-x" commits that clutter the history.

**Cons:**
*   **Rewriting History:** You are technically creating *new* commits with different hashes. This is dangerous on shared branches.
*   **Conflict Fatigue:** If you rebase a branch with 20 commits, and a conflict occurs, you may have to resolve the same conflict 20 times (once for each commit being re-applied).

---

### Comparison Summary

| Feature | Git Merge | Git Rebase |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **History Type** | Preserves actual history (Non-linear) | Creates a clean, artificial history (Linear) |
| **Commit Hashes** | Keeps original hashes | Creates new hashes for re-applied commits |
| **Complexity** | Simple, one-step resolution | Can be complex; requires manual re-application |
| **Traceability** | High (shows exactly what happened) | Low (hides the fact that work was done in parallel) |

---

### Team Workflow Recommendation: The "Golden Rule"

The biggest risk in a team setting is the **Golden Rule of Rebasing**: 
> **Never rebase branches that have been pushed to a public/shared repository.**

If you rebase a branch that others are working on, you are effectively "deleting" the history they are working on and replacing it with a new one. When they try to pull, their Git will be confused, resulting in massive merge conflicts and duplicate commits.

#### The "Best of Both Worlds" Workflow
Most professional teams use a hybrid approach:

1.  **While working locally (Private):** Use `git rebase` to keep your local feature branch up-to-date with `main`. This keeps your work clean and avoids "noise" commits.
2.  **When finished (Public):** Use `git merge` (specifically a "Squash and Merge" via GitHub/GitLab) to pull the feature into the `main` branch.

**The Result:** Your individual feature is a clean, single commit on the main timeline, but the messy "work-in-progress" commits from your local machine are never seen by your teammates.
```
